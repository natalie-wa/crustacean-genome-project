About the project

Crustacean genomes for biodiversity science

Crustaceans offer a powerful comparative framework for understanding evolution, adaptation, and ecosystem function. The Crustacean Genome Project brings researchers together around a shared goal of sequencing genomes from 1,000 crustacean species, building resources that reflect the breadth of this diversity and support research across disciplines.

The scientific case

Why crustaceans?

Crustaceans are central to some of the most consequential questions in animal biology. Their evolutionary relationships connect the origins of insects with the diversification of aquatic arthropods. Their repeated transitions between environments make it possible to investigate how adaptation evolves. Their ecological roles connect genomic research with the functioning and resilience of ecosystems.

Understanding arthropod evolution requires crustacean diversity. Insects are nested within Pancrustacea, the evolutionary group that also includes traditionally recognized crustaceans. A comparative framework built around insects and only a few crustacean relatives leaves important branches of that history poorly represented. Broader crustacean sampling strengthens the evidence available for reconstructing relationships and investigating the evolution of body plans, development, and biological function. [1]

Repeated evolutionary transitions create powerful comparisons. Different crustacean lineages have independently adapted to life on land, while parasitic groups have undergone striking changes in body form. These transitions allow researchers to test whether similar biological outcomes involve shared genomic mechanisms or different evolutionary routes. Those tests require multiple independent origins and the appropriate relatives for comparison. [2] [3]

Their biology matters beyond the genome. Antarctic krill are central to Southern Ocean food webs, while herbivorous Caribbean king crabs can reduce macroalgae and support coral reef recovery. These examples connect the study of crustacean biology to ecological processes and conservation. Genomic resources provide a foundation for investigating the mechanisms underlying those roles. [4] [5]

The value of sequencing another crustacean therefore depends on the comparisons it makes possible. Expanding into poorly represented lineages can strengthen entire research programs by adding the evolutionary and ecological context that isolated genomes cannot provide.

The foundation

What is a reference genome?

A genome assembly reconstructs DNA sequence from sequencing data. A reference genome provides a sequence framework for locating genes, comparing DNA, and organizing subsequent research. Annotation adds information about genes and other features, making the sequence easier to investigate and reuse. [6]

One assembly does not capture all variation within a species. Its usefulness also depends on sequence accuracy, completeness, contamination screening, and reliable metadata. Long scaffolds are valuable, but their lengths alone cannot establish that an assembly is complete or correctly organized. [7]

Research opportunities

From genomic resources to biological insight

Genome architecture

The enormous, repeat-rich Antarctic krill genome demonstrates how much crustacean genome organization can differ from familiar model systems. Comparisons across lineages can help explain genome expansion and distinguish shared features from lineage-specific changes. [4]

Social evolution

Sponge-dwelling snapping shrimps offer an opportunity to study eusociality outside insects. Research linking social organization with genome size and repetitive DNA illustrates the biological questions opened by genomic approaches in less familiar research systems. [8]

Ecology and conservation

Reference genomes can support studies of population variation, history, and responses to environmental change. Their ecological value grows when genomic research is paired with knowledge of species biology, habitat, and management needs. [9]

The approach

Build a comparative resource together

CrustGP aims to coordinate how genomic resources are developed across the community. Its priorities are to identify informative sampling gaps, connect expertise and specimens with sequencing opportunities, and promote quality assessment, annotation, and specimen-linked metadata that make resources useful across studies.

Taxonomists, museum researchers, and regional experts contribute essential knowledge of identification, diversity, and specimen context. Ecologists, conservation researchers, and fisheries and aquaculture scientists help define the biological questions. Genomicists and bioinformaticians connect those questions with data generation, evaluation, and analysis.

Success requires both broader representation and resources that others can find, interpret, and reuse. Shared data practices, reproducible workflows, and opportunities for training can help make individual sequencing efforts cumulative. CrustGP is intended to complement existing databases and research initiatives by supporting the coordination that begins before a genome reaches a repository.

From specimen to resource

What goes into a useful genome?

Sequencing is one part of a longer process. The steps below describe common components of biodiversity genome projects, rather than a single protocol that fits every species. [10] [7]

  1. Identify and document

    Establish the organism’s identity and record its specimen context. Link the sequence to a voucher or other traceable material where feasible.

  2. Collect and preserve

    Plan collection, preservation, and handling around the material needed for sequencing. DNA quality can be affected before the sample reaches a laboratory.

  3. Sequence and assemble

    Generate sequence data and reconstruct the genome. Where suitable data are available, additional methods can help organize assembled sequences into larger structures.

  4. Evaluate the assembly

    Assess contiguity, completeness, accuracy, contamination, and duplicated sequence. Several complementary measures are needed to understand assembly quality.

  5. Annotate and describe

    Identify genes and other sequence features, recording methods and supporting evidence so that researchers can interpret the annotation.

  6. Deposit and connect

    Make sequences and metadata accessible through public repositories. Connect accessions with specimens, methods, and publications to support discovery and reuse.

Explore the evidence

Making representation visible

The Genome Explorer brings together the project’s existing assembly inventory with searchable NCBI links and summaries of taxonomic representation. It is a starting point for finding resources and identifying groups that need closer attention.

Assembly records, biological genomes, and accepted species are different counting units. The explorer preserves those distinctions and identifies records needing review. As the inventory and taxonomic reference improve, the dashboard can support more detailed assessments of coverage.
